1 The Network on Chip
1.1 Topology and addressing
The network is a 4 × 4 mesh of sixteen routers, each with one PE attached. A router and its PE share
an address, the coordinate pair (𝑥, 𝑦): 𝑥 is the column, counted from 0 at the left, and 𝑦 is the row,
counted from 0 at the top. The top-left router is therefore (0, 0) and the bottom-right router is (3, 3). Each
coordinate is a 2-bit number.
Directions are named as on a map, with north at the top. For the router at (𝑥, 𝑦):
• the north port connects to (𝑥, 𝑦 − 1) and the south port to (𝑥, 𝑦 + 1);
• the west port connects to (𝑥 − 1, 𝑦) and the east port to (𝑥 + 1, 𝑦).
A port that would point off the grid — the north ports of the top row, the west ports of the left column, and
so on — is unused. Nothing will ever arrive on it, and your router must never send anything out of it.

3 Functional specification
3.1 What the test bench guarantees
We understand that developing a full fledged and efficient NoC is a hard task, and are providing you with
some guarantees to make it easier. We expect your design to hold up against these requirements and our
testbench will never violate any of these. While you are free to explore and build the NoC further on your
own, we will limit the scope to these requirements alone. We however recommend that you think about
these in detail as architectural and functional thinking is a very useful skill to pick up!
A1. A packet put in at node (𝑥, 𝑦) has src = (𝑥, 𝑦).
A2. dst is a node of the mesh, and never equal to src.
A3. Each PE puts in the packets of a message consecutively, in increasing order of seq_num from 0.
A4. Each PE has at most one message in the network at a time. It puts in the first packet of its next
message only after every packet of the current message has been collected at the destination.
A5. Every packet that arrives at a node’s local port is eventually collected, although a node may stop
collecting for a long time.
A3 and A4 together mean that no two packets in the network at the same time share the same src, dst and
seq_num. Those three fields identify a packet uniquely, so a reorder buffer can tell packets apart by them
alone.
3.2 What your network must guarantee
G1. Delivery. Every packet accepted at a node’s local port is returned exactly once, at the local port of
the node named in its dst field, identical in all 268 bits to the packet that was put in. No packet may
be lost, duplicated, corrupted or delivered to the wrong node.
G2. Ordering. For every pair of nodes 𝑆 and 𝐷, the packets that 𝑆 sends to 𝐷 are returned at 𝐷 in the
order in which 𝑆 put them in. Note that the source is guaranteed to put the packets in ascending
order of sequence numbers, should there be any. This means you are expected to output the packets
in the same order. This is a hard requirement. While the packets themselves can get scrambled
inside the NoC, your design must put them in order before it leaves the output port.
G3. Progress. Whatever the traffic, every accepted packet is eventually delivered, and every PE that is

3.3 Interface and timing contract
The local port follows the same discipline as the interface of Assignment 1, and the test bench relies on it.
Contract
C1. ma_put_packet may fire only when the router can genuinely accept the packet. Its RDY is your
router’s “full” signal towards the PE: when there is no room, the method is simply not ready,
and the PE waits.
C2. mav_get_packet may fire only when a packet for that node is genuinely available. It must never
return a stale, duplicated or partially assembled packet.
C3. Packets are returned in the order that G2 requires.
C4. Both conditions above are expressed as implicit conditions, so that the generated RDY signals
carry them. Do not attach (* always_ready *) or (* always_enabled *) to either method, and
do not build an unguarded handshake in which a separate method reports validity or fullness.
C1–C4 apply to the local port only. Between routers, you may use whatever handshake you like.
The test bench applies back-pressure on both sides. It will sometimes put packets in on consecutive cycles,
as fast as ma_put_packet allows, and it will sometimes stop collecting at a node for many cycles. A packet
whose destination is not collecting must wait inside the network, never be dropped, and back-pressure will
then spread upstream; when the node starts collecting again, the network must drain.
3.4 Extreme load test
To make the assignment more challenging and to incentivize extensive thinking, there will be a
specific amount of points alloted for performance under high load. For many thousands of cycles, all
sixteen PEs will hammer the NoC with messages, as soon as the routers are ready to accept it. This
will test if your design is robust to different kinds of workloads and if you honored the guarantees
outlined above. A network that loses/duplicates/reorders/deadlocks/stalls under this load will not be
awarded these points.

4 Performance
Alongside a working network, you must estimate how much bandwidth your router can support and
how your network performs, and then measure both. We will measure the same quantities on your
submission with our own traffic generators and compare them with yours. Table 4 defines them. There is
no clock-period model in this assignment, so everything is counted in clock cycles, and bandwidth counts
payload bytes only: header bits are overhead.
The bandwidth by area (B/W) fraction will have to be estimated after PnR(Placement and Routing
process) with the actual clock rate and reported in the assignment. This will be a measure of how
efficient your design is.

6 Constraints
1. Do not modify NocTypes.bsv.
2. In Noc.bsv, the package name, the module name, its interface and its (* synthesize *) attribute
are fixed; the test bench will not compile against changed ones. You may add imports and helper
functions.
3. mkNoc should do nothing but instantiate routers from your NocRouter package, one per node, and
connect them to each other and to node. Anything each node needs, such as a reorder buffer, belongs
in the router package.
4. NocRouter.bsv holds package NocRouter, imports only NocTypes and packages that ship with bsc,
and compiles on its own. The names of the router module and its interface are your choice.
5. Size everything from the typedefs in NocTypes.bsv, not from the numbers they currently hold.
6. The design must be synthesisable, and mkNoc must compile through the Verilog flow.

7 Building and testing
mkNoc is a concrete module, so you can check that it compiles to Verilog on its own:
$ mkdir -p build
$ bsc -u -verilog -bdir build -vdir build -info-dir build -g mkNoc Noc.bsv

To simulate it, write a test bench that instantiates it and compile that for Bluesim:
$ bsc -u -sim -bdir build -simdir build -info-dir build -g mkTbNoc TbNoc.bsv
$ bsc -sim -bdir build -simdir build -e mkTbNoc -o simTbNoc
$ ./simTbNoc

You must write and submit two test benches:
1. a router test bench, which exercises one router on its own. It drives the router’s ports directly and
checks that each packet leaves by the output your routing policy chooses, that contention for an output
loses nothing, and that back-pressure on an output stalls the right inputs; and
2. a NoC test bench, which drives mkNoc through Ifc_noc and checks G1–G3.
The NoC test bench must include, at the least, a single packet sent from (0, 0) to (3, 3) with nothing else
in the network: the packet must arrive at (3, 3), unchanged. You are free to explore further tests. Note that
having high confidence on your NoC router will hugely reduce the possibility of having a faulty NoC itself.
The README.md file attached with the assignment package provides you with directions along with a
basic starter testbench. This is so that you can understand how inputs would be driven in the evaluation
tests. While you are free to modify that testbench to include your own tests, it is recommended that you
adopt your own testbenches, while ensuring it adheres to the framework followed in the sample.
